Abstract

Background. Diabetic retinopathy (DR) screening is increasingly automated, but deployed deep learning systems for DR severity grading typically commit to a label for every image, with no principled mechanism for abstention on uncertain cases. Selective prediction — abstaining when uncertainty is high and deferring to a human reader — is the natural fit, yet the literature on DR uncertainty quantification is fragmented across binary referable / non-referable formulations, varied backbones, and inconsistent evaluation harnesses.

Method. We train a single EfficientNet-B0 baseline on APTOS 2019 with a locked random seed and evaluate four post-hoc uncertainty methods on the identical held-out test set: (i) plain softmax confidence, (ii) Temperature Scaling, (iii) MC Dropout with T=30 stochastic forward passes (yielding four uncertainty signals: negative max-confidence, predictive entropy, expected entropy, mutual information), and (iv) Split Conformal Prediction with two variants (set-size and APS), all at α=0.10. Every method is evaluated through the same risk-coverage harness and the same per-bin calibration analysis.

Results. The deterministic baseline achieves test accuracy 0.809 and quadratic-weighted κ 0.865. Temperature Scaling reduces Expected Calibration Error from 0.146 to 0.055 (62% relative reduction) with a single fitted scalar T=2.25, at the cost of a small AURC increase. MC Dropout's mutual-information signal (BALD) achieves the lowest AURC of any method, 0.0756, beating both deterministic softmax (0.0793) and Temperature Scaling (0.0852). Split Conformal attains empirical coverage 0.902 against a 0.90 target with average prediction-set size 1.35 (76.0% singletons). Three different methods, three different wins: calibration, selective-prediction quality, and coverage guarantee.

Contribution. This report is positioned as an open, reproducible benchmark rather than a novel methodological contribution. All training is from a single Kaggle notebook, all per-sample outputs are saved as CSVs, all weights & biases are exposed via a public W&B run, and the exact git commit producing each number is recorded inside the result files. The release accompanies the source code at github.com/ShahnawazKakarh/retinal-selective-prediction.

1. Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy is the leading cause of preventable adult blindness worldwide. Modern deep learning systems for DR severity grading routinely achieve quadratic-weighted κ in the 0.85–0.94 range on the APTOS 2019 benchmark [1], yet the gap between competition leaderboard performance and clinical deployability remains wide. A clinical workflow does not need a single prediction; it needs a prediction plus a defensible reason to either trust that prediction or escalate to a human reviewer. This is the selective-prediction problem.

The literature has matured rapidly on the technical side. Temperature Scaling [2] demonstrated that post-hoc calibration with a single scalar yields large reductions in Expected Calibration Error. MC Dropout [3] connected dropout-at-inference to approximate Bayesian inference. Conformal Prediction [4][5] gave distribution-free finite-sample coverage guarantees. Risk-coverage analysis [6][7] formalized how to compare uncertainty signals against each other independently of the underlying model's accuracy. Each individual technique is well-understood.

What remains awkward, in our view, is the integration. Published benchmarks on DR uncertainty typically use different backbones, different splits, different signals, and different evaluation scripts — making head-to-head comparison difficult. Filos et al. [8] and Band et al. [9] are notable exceptions but focus on the EyePACS / Kaggle DR dataset rather than APTOS 2019, and target the binary referable task rather than full 5-class ordinal grading. The smaller, newer APTOS dataset has a distinct label distribution and is the de-facto benchmark for the 5-class formulation.

This report provides a clean, reproducible, single-seed, single-backbone evaluation of four post-hoc uncertainty methods on APTOS 2019. We deliberately do not claim a new method; we provide a controlled comparison that other researchers can copy, modify, and extend.

Scope clarification. This is version 1.0.0 of an ongoing project. The novel methodological piece — class-conditional selective thresholds for ordinal DR grading — together with external validation on IDRiD and Messidor-2, Deep Ensembles, and Evidential Deep Learning, are explicitly scoped for v1.1.0 and v2.0.0. They are not part of this release. We make this distinction up front to avoid the appearance of over-claiming.

2. Related Work

2.1 Uncertainty quantification for medical imaging

Bayesian deep learning approaches to medical imaging classification are surveyed in [10]. For diabetic retinopathy specifically, Leibig et al. [11] were among the first to apply MC Dropout to a large-scale DR screening dataset, demonstrating that referring high-uncertainty samples to a human reader improves the kept-class accuracy. Ayhan et al. [12] extended this with test-time augmentation. Linmans et al. [13] evaluated Evidential Deep Learning on retinal imaging.

2.2 Selective prediction

El-Yaniv and Wiener [6] formalized selective classification and the risk-coverage curve. Geifman and El-Yaniv [7] introduced SelectiveNet and the AURC / Excess-AURC metrics that we use throughout this report. Recent work has extended selective prediction to large language models [14] and to distribution shift settings [15].

2.3 Conformal prediction

Conformal Prediction [16] provides distribution-free finite-sample coverage guarantees. The Split Conformal variant [4] is the practical choice for deep learning; Adaptive Prediction Sets (APS) [17] improve set-size efficiency on imbalanced problems. Angelopoulos and Bates [5] provide a tutorial.

2.4 Calibration

Modern neural networks are systematically over-confident [2]. Temperature Scaling fits a single scalar T to the validation logits via maximum likelihood; this preserves the argmax and therefore the accuracy, while dramatically improving calibration as measured by Expected Calibration Error [18]. Recent work questions the adequacy of ECE itself [19] and proposes alternatives; we report ECE in this release for comparability with prior work, while keeping the per-sample outputs available so future analyses can recompute other calibration metrics.

3. Methods

3.1 Backbone and training

We train EfficientNet-B0 [20] initialized from ImageNet-pretrained weights. The classification head is a single linear layer over 5 outputs with a dropout layer (p=0.3) immediately before it — the same dropout layer is reused at inference time for MC Dropout. The image preprocessing pipeline (Albumentations) follows standard practice for DR: resize-with-aspect-ratio to 224 then pad to 224×224, ImageNet normalization, random horizontal and vertical flips, mild color jitter, and a small probability of Gaussian noise.

Training uses AdamW (learning rate 3e-4, weight decay 1e-2) with cosine annealing and 1 epoch of linear warmup. Loss is standard cross-entropy. We train for up to 30 epochs with mixed-precision (AMP) and early stopping (patience 7) on validation quadratic-weighted κ. The full training configuration is committed at configs/baseline.yaml and saved alongside each checkpoint.

3.2 Splits

The 3,662 labeled APTOS training images are split into train / val / test = 70 / 15 / 15 with a stratified shuffle. The split is fully deterministic from GLOBAL_SEED=42. The same splits are used for all uncertainty methods.

3.3 Uncertainty methods

3.3.1 Plain softmax (deterministic baseline)

For each test sample, we use the negative of the max-softmax probability as the uncertainty signal. Predictive entropy is also computed for comparison. No calibration, no abstention by default; an abstention rule is added post-hoc via the risk-coverage harness.

3.3.2 Temperature Scaling

A single scalar T is fitted on the validation logits by minimizing NLL with L-BFGS. The temperature is parameterized via a softplus to keep T > 0 throughout optimization. At inference we divide test logits by T before softmax. ECE is recomputed on the calibrated test probabilities.

3.3.3 MC Dropout (T=30)

We perform 30 stochastic forward passes with dropout active. From the T softmax distributions we compute:

  • Predictive entropy: H[E_t[p_t]] — total uncertainty.
  • Expected entropy: E_t[H[p_t]] — aleatoric component.
  • Mutual information (BALD): H[E_t[p_t]] − E_t[H[p_t]] — epistemic component.
  • Negative max confidence: −max E_t[p_t] — for direct comparison with the deterministic baseline.

All four signals are saved per sample for downstream analysis.

3.3.4 Conformal Prediction

We implement both Split Conformal (non-conformity score = 1 − softmax probability of the true class on val) and Adaptive Prediction Sets (APS). The non-conformity threshold q̂ is calibrated to satisfy a target coverage of 1 − α = 0.90 on the validation set, and applied unchanged to the test set. We report empirical coverage, average set size, median set size, and singleton fraction.

3.4 Evaluation

For each method we compute, on the held-out test set: accuracy, quadratic-weighted Cohen's κ, NLL, Brier score, Expected Calibration Error (15 bins, equal-width), per-class precision / recall / F1, the risk-coverage curve, AURC (area under the risk-coverage curve), Excess-AURC (AURC minus the optimal-ranking baseline), and selective accuracy at coverage levels 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%. For Conformal we additionally report empirical coverage and prediction-set diagnostics.

Excess-AURC is the key metric for isolating the quality of the uncertainty signal from the underlying classifier's accuracy. Two methods can have similar AURC if one has high accuracy and a weak uncertainty signal while another has lower accuracy and a strong uncertainty signal; Excess-AURC separates these.

6. Discussion

Three different methods, three different wins. The cleanest takeaway from this benchmark is that the choice of uncertainty method should follow the downstream objective:

  • If the deployment needs calibrated probabilities (e.g., for cost-sensitive triage), Temperature Scaling is the obvious choice. It is essentially free, preserves the argmax, and roughly halves ECE.
  • If the deployment needs the best abstention policy (i.e., the highest accuracy on retained predictions for any given coverage), MC Dropout's mutual-information signal wins. The cost is 30× inference compute.
  • If the deployment needs a coverage guarantee (e.g., a regulator requires "at least 90% of cases must have the correct label in the returned set"), Split Conformal is the only method here that provides one. Its average set size of 1.35 is operationally reasonable.

Notably, raw selective accuracy at 80% coverage is essentially identical across all methods (0.882 – 0.884). The differences live in AURC and ECE, not in selective accuracy at any single coverage level. This is consistent with prior observations that risk-coverage curves are dominated by the underlying classifier and that uncertainty methods can shift the curve only modestly.

The most clinically actionable finding is the per-class behavior of the baseline: F1 of 0.48 on Mild and 0.45 on Severe, against 0.97 on No DR. A global confidence threshold for abstention will disproportionately abstain on these minority classes — the wrong direction. The class-conditional selective threshold method we plan to add in v1.1.0 directly addresses this by setting per-class abstention rules calibrated to equalize per-class retained recall.

7. Limitations and Future Work

Limitations of this release. We make these explicit to set expectations honestly.
  1. Single seed. All numbers reported are from a single training run with seed 42. We do not estimate run-to-run variance. The next version will report mean ± standard deviation across at least 3 seeds.
  2. Single dataset, internal split only. All evaluation is on a held-out portion of APTOS 2019 itself. We do not test generalization to other DR datasets. External validation on IDRiD and Messidor-2 is scoped for v1.1.0.
  3. Single backbone. EfficientNet-B0 was chosen for its strong accuracy / compute trade-off, but we do not compare to ResNet-50, ConvNeXt, or ViT backbones. Whether our findings generalize across backbones is open.
  4. Methods omitted. Deep Ensembles and Evidential Deep Learning are well-known strong baselines that are not included in this version, primarily because Deep Ensembles requires M independent training runs (compute cost) and Evidential DL requires a separate training pipeline with a different loss.
  5. ECE has known weaknesses. ECE depends on binning and can hide important calibration failures. We save per-sample softmax outputs so future analyses can recompute Brier decomposition, adaptive-bin ECE, and reliability via density-estimation methods.
  6. 5-class ordinal structure is not exploited. We treat DR grading as flat 5-class classification. Quadratic-weighted κ partially compensates by penalizing off-by-one errors more lightly than off-by-three errors, but our uncertainty methods themselves are oblivious to ordinality. An ordinality-aware uncertainty method is a natural extension.

7.1 Concrete plan for v1.1.0

  • Add IDRiD external validation. The IDRiD grading scheme matches APTOS's 5-class ICDR, so no re-mapping is required.
  • Introduce class-conditional selective thresholds — the novel methodological piece. Two variants: equalized-recall thresholds, and ordinal-distance-weighted thresholds.
  • Report mean ± SD across 3 seeds.

7.2 Concrete plan for v2.0.0

  • Add Deep Ensembles (M=5) and Evidential Deep Learning.
  • Add Messidor-2 external validation (Google's adjudicated 5-class re-grading).
  • Add an equity audit — do abstention rates correlate with image-quality proxies? With patient demographic strata where available?
  • Submit the consolidated work to IEEE Journal of Biomedical and Health Informatics (J-BHI).

8. Conclusion

We have presented a small, honest, fully reproducible benchmark of four post-hoc uncertainty quantification methods on APTOS 2019 diabetic retinopathy grading. The release accompanies open-source code, locked seeds, a public Weights & Biases run, and a Kaggle notebook that reproduces every number in this report end-to-end in about an hour on a single T4 GPU.

The clearest conclusion is that different uncertainty methods optimize for different downstream objectives: Temperature Scaling for calibration, MC Dropout with mutual information for selective-prediction quality, Split Conformal for coverage guarantees. There is no single best method; there is a per-deployment best choice. The per-class behavior of the baseline classifier suggests that the most impactful next methodological step is not another generic uncertainty method, but a class-conditional abstention rule tailored to ordinal DR severity grading. That is the focus of v1.1.0.
